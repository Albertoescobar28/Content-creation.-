# Hook Frameworks

## How Briar teaches hooks

Briar treats hooks as a psychology problem, not a template problem. His recurring stance is that plug-and-play hook frameworks flop because audiences have a low tolerance for anything that feels inauthentic, and that tolerance is only getting lower as AI and scripted content saturate feeds. His two most-repeated warnings: "avoid hook frameworks, lean on psychological principles instead" (2025-10-20) and "hooks are less important than buzzwords and topics are" (2025-12-16). He consistently ranks the ingredients of a video as topic first, format second, hook third, illustration fourth (2026-02-20, 2026-04-22, 2026-06-24), calling ideas "king" and framing the hook as the amplifier of a good idea rather than a rescue for a bad one.

He also insists a hook is not one sentence. He teaches "five hooks to every video" (verbal, on-screen text, visual, caption, format) operating at once (2025-10-20), and pushes creators to think of the hook as multiple short beats rather than a single line (2025-11-20). Curiosity/anticipation is the emotion he returns to most, calling it "the greatest emotion you should be trying to evoke in content" (2025-10-20) and "the 80/20 rule of content" (2026-07-06).

## Named frameworks

### HF1 — Pain Point + Dream Outcome + Curiosity + Social Proof (with a Pump Fake)
**Definition**: Briar's default high-leverage hook stack, the one he says he'd use for the rest of his life if forced to pick one.
**Structure**: Snipe a specific observable pain point, name the dream outcome the viewer actually wants (positioning the video as the bridge between the two), layer in social proof (which can come from books, studies, or numbers, not just yourself), and let curiosity be baked in by the structure. A pump fake is layered on top to break the expected pattern.
**Real example**: "If you're tired of the 3 hitter... and you actually want to K them, you want to make them look absolutely stupid, then this is the video for you." (2025-10-24, pain-to-dream bridge)
**Sources**: 2025-10-24, 2025-10-27

### HF2 — The URS Trifecta (High TAM + Specific Observable Circumstance + Unique Framing)
**Definition**: The intersection that produces top-of-funnel viral hooks. The viral emotion Briar chases is "oh, that's what that was."
**Structure**: (1) A total addressable market so large you ask "could billions watch this," (2) a very specific observable circumstance or emotion the viewer instantly recognizes, that a huge number of people have felt but nobody articulates, and (3) a unique framing they've never heard before. Bonus points if you're the first person to make them feel that way.
**Real example**: One of Briar's best videos ever was about "poor follower quality," a term no one had used before, cited as high TAM plus specific emotion plus a unique name. (2025-09-22)
**Sources**: 2025-09-17, 2025-09-22, 2025-09-24

### HF3 — Hook → Super Hook → Explain → Illustrate → Apply
**Definition**: Briar's foundational body-and-hook blueprint for when you're "brain dead and need to record." The super hook is the second beat right after the hook that cements why the viewer must commit.
**Structure**: Hook (open loop), super hook (cast the viewer into the emotional stakes / cement why this problem matters, often with a credibility tag or pain point), explain (1-3 sentences of your thesis without giving the climax), illustrate (8-10 sentences of story, metaphor, or study), apply (one hyper-tactical, unique action step). The Explain-Illustrate-Apply chunk can be repeated for listicles. Briar says this should be no more than a third of your videos.
**Real example**: The super hook framework came from Daavi Gazelle in Ben Bader's program; Briar applied it the next week and got a client a 1M-view video. Daavi's demo super hook on a feng shui video (dramatized by Briar): "You're gonna probably slip outside your door and break your leg, your man's going to break up with you, and someone's going to steal $8,000 from you." (2026-04-03)
**Sources**: 2025-12-10, 2025-12-17, 2026-04-03, 2026-05-11

### HF4 — The Whisper Technique (parenthetical curiosity loop)
**Definition**: A short parenthetical "whisper" placed under the main on-screen hook, hitting one emotional pillar.
**Structure**: 2-3 words in parentheses under the main 8-word hook, hitting one of three pillars: curiosity (acronyms, named methods), risk reversal (preempt the viewer's objection about time or applicability), or social proof (credibility numbers).
**Real example**: Briar's most viral videos run "about eight words on the on screen text, not including the parentheses below" (2025-09-17). His delayed-payoff "Huberman method" on-screen hook is a cited example of the curiosity-whisper approach.
**Sources**: 2025-09-17, 2025-11-19, 2025-11-20

### HF5 — Pump Fake (pattern break mid-hook)
**Definition**: Setting the viewer's mental imagery in one direction, then redirecting it to create a plot twist that lifts retention.
**Structure**: Two reliable pump-fake sentences: "But this is where it gets crazy" and "You might think it's X, but it's actually Y." Briar calls "But this is where it gets crazy" one of the best sentences you can say in content.
**Real example**: A Callaway video about Taylor Swift and the NFL used a pump fake claiming the tech industry (AWS, ESPN) was actually the biggest beneficiary, not the NFL itself. (2025-10-24)
**Sources**: 2025-10-24, 2025-10-27

### HF6 — Contrast Hook
**Definition**: Positioning the video in the middle of two opposing concepts, which is inherently watchable. Briar says weaving contrast into a hook produces an outlier "100% of the time" across his clients.
**Structure**: Stack contrast into the hook (small vs. large, good vs. bad, before vs. after, identity vs. identity). To get followers (not just views) from contrast, you must pick a side, because people follow opinions and stances.
**Real example**: Avni's Mike and Ike visual hook (one candy = millionaire, a pile = billionaire), communicating scale contrast in roughly four seconds. (2025-10-24, 2025-10-13)
**Sources**: 2025-10-06, 2025-10-24

### HF7 — Identity-Driven Hook (identities over verbs)
**Definition**: Framing the two sides of a comparison or split-screen as identities the viewer can claim, not actions or verbs.
**Structure**: Use claimable titles ("practice owner" vs. "associate") instead of verbs ("reactive" vs. "strategic"), because identity makes the viewer ask "which one am I?"
**Real example**: "'Practice owner', that's a title that I can go own." (2026-02-05, contrasted against verb-framed hooks)
**Sources**: 2026-02-04, 2026-02-05

### HF8 — Fear-Based Hook (relatable pain point + fear + resolution)
**Definition**: A stable long-term hook play that, unlike fascination marketing, does not require constant escalation.
**Structure**: Lead with a relatable pain point (the real source of the power), add fear layered on top, twist the knife, then leave the viewer with a solution or something positive at the end. Cap it at roughly 50% of your content.
**Real example**: Danny Fisher's contribution, endorsed by Briar: "If you can scare people in the first 5 to 10 seconds, and you lean into a problem and go into the twist the knife, as long as you can give some sort of solution or leave the viewer with something positive at the end, you'll get really good conversions." (2025-12-30)
**Sources**: 2025-12-29, 2025-12-30

### HF9 — Address and Dress Up the Elephant in the Room
**Definition**: A copywriting move where you point out the obvious unspoken reality nobody is naming, then make it look beautiful. Attributed to great copywriters.
**Structure**: State the elephant (the obvious that no one is talking about), then dress it up so it lands as unique and compelling.
**Real example**: Grant Owen's lawn video hook: "If you cut your own grass, you're announcing to the neighborhood that you're broke." (2026-03-23, cited as this type of hook by Grant; principle taught by Briar 2026-07-06)
**Sources**: 2026-07-06

### HF10 — Emotional Resonance Hook (curiosity-free virality)
**Definition**: A hook that goes viral by striking a perfect emotional nerve rather than opening a curiosity loop. Harder to hit reliably, so Briar says lean on curiosity first.
**Structure**: Strike a perfect emotional overlap with what the audience wants to dialogue on. No open loop required; the resonance itself drives comments and shares.
**Real example**: Richard Yu's hook "you should never harm your mother" had zero curiosity but strong emotional resonance; a related video, "the people who never challenge you don't believe you can do better," hit 600,000 views on emotional resonance. (2026-07-06)
**Sources**: 2026-07-06

## Hook rules and constraints

- **On-screen hooks run ~8 words; nine is the hard max.** Briar's most viral videos average about eight words on the main on-screen hook (2025-09-17). Later refined: nine words is the ceiling, with a steep falloff past it, based on an email-subject-line study he treats as transferable (2025-11-19, 2025-11-20).
- **Two lines of on-screen text beats three.** Two-line hooks tend to outperform three-line hooks; TikTok's gauge tool can enforce this (2025-09-08). Never use three lines unless broken up with indentation or parentheses (2025-10-31). A three-line vs. two-line difference produced a 5x view swing in Jefferson Fisher / Chris Williamson testing (2026-05-06).
- **Never repeat the on-screen hook in the spoken hook.** The mind processes reading and listening simultaneously, so the spoken line must add or compress, never echo. Repeating is "one of the biggest mistakes" creators make (2025-09-08). The on-screen text should be an open loop that alludes to something delivered later, never a restatement (2026-06-12).
- **On-screen text and verbal hook should each cover a different chunk of the video.** Aim to allude to as much of the video as possible in the first 3-5 seconds by having the verbal hook hit one part and the on-screen text hit another (2025-05-08). Don't overlap what you communicate across the five hooks 90% of the time (2025-10-20).
- **Balance clarity and curiosity across the two hooks.** They are contrasting opposites; whichever you lead with in one, lean the other way in the second. Never sacrifice clarity entirely (2025-09-17).
- **Resolve the first hook sentence in under ~8 seconds; start your first sentence within 3 seconds.** Eight seconds to finish the opening sentence is too slow (2025-10-27). On reaction/commentary videos especially, start talking before the 3-second mark (2026-02-05).
- **Hook window scales with account size.** Under 1K followers: ~1.5 seconds. 5K-20K: ~3.75 seconds. 15K-100K: 5-7 seconds, because credibility buys attention (2025-10-20).
- **Max three focal points / attractions on screen at once.** Borrowed from Paddy Galloway's YouTube thumbnail rule; more than three becomes noise the brain skips (2025-11-19, 2025-11-20, 2026-01-02).
- **Get the most important word early in the sentence.** Filler words at the front cost you the 1-2 second retention cliff. Move the keyword, number, or pronoun that carries the punch as close to word one as possible (2025-10-24).
- **Go noun-crazy, cut function words.** Punchy nouns (especially proper nouns) and numbers catch the eye; capitalization should land on the meaningful noun, not filler like "how to" (2025-10-31). Keep function words (is, the, has, your, not, if, but) under ~30-40% of on-screen words; higher function-word counts correlated with worse performance in Briar's own data (2025-11-21, 2025-11-22, 2026-01-02).
- **"How," "what," and "when" beat "why" and "who."** Action-step framings outperform definition/philosophy framings; people want tactics, not philosophy (2025-10-27, 2025-10-31).
- **Make hooks tangible.** Kill vague words like "things," "better," "more," "great," "success," "improve." Replace with concrete units (steps, frameworks, questions, habits), specific numbers, or observable moments. Dollar amounts are the "bottom of the barrel" form of tangibility; the harder, better work is the emotion beneath the money (2025-10-20, 2025-10-23, 2025-10-24).
- **Make claims falsifiable and pointable.** Every sentence should pass three copywriting questions: Is it falsifiable? Can I see/touch it? Has no one else said this? "Success" is unfalsifiable and should leave your vocabulary. Harry Dry won't make an ad if he can't physically point at the thing (2025-11-21, 2025-11-22).
- **Speak at a fifth-grade reading level.** Overwhelm is a top reason viewers bounce; losing them on two words causes a scroll. Simple language still converts sophisticated, high-ticket buyers, and speaking simply takes more intelligence (2025-09-17, 2025-10-24, 2025-11-21).
- **Write hooks like a text, not a memo.** The closer the tone reads like a text message between friends (lowercase, "ppl," "lol"), the better it performed; formal tone hurt performance (2025-11-19, 2025-11-20).
- **Hold on-screen text ~7-10 seconds, not the 3-second app default.** Nobody scrolls because text stayed up too long; extending it gives the eye time to journey across face, captions, and hook (2026-02-04, 2026-02-05, 2025-05-08, 2026-05-22).
- **Curiosity is a dial, not a switch.** Turned up too far is clickbait; turned down too far is implied or zero curiosity. Aim for the sweet spot (2026-07-06).
- **Reopen the curiosity loop immediately after you pay it off.** The moment you deliver the answer, the viewer is gone unless you open a new loop in the same beat. Rehooks only need one or two sentences to reinvigorate curiosity (2025-09-17, 2025-09-24, 2025-10-23, 2025-12-10). "But" is the single most powerful word for reopening loops (2025-12-15, 2025-12-17).
- **Reusing a proven hook is acceptable, capped at ~once a week.** Apply the 3% rule: tweak, don't rewrite. Hooks that tried too hard to reinvent the wheel underperformed (2025-11-19, 2025-11-20, 2026-05-22).
- **Close the curiosity loop ~80%, leave 10-20% open.** Give the viewer the sense they got almost everything but still need the last piece (referenced by members from a Briar call, 2026-04-24).
- **Topic novelty beats hook craft.** The uniqueness of the buzzword/concept in the first few seconds matters more than the phrasing. "Avoidance audit" landed because it was novel; "friendship audit" flopped (2025-12-16). Ideas outrank hooks; Kolby's keyword-list video had no hook at all and still earned ~2,500 followers in under 24 hours (2026-07-06).
- **Diagnose hook problems with skip rate.** A 45-50% skip rate at the 3-second mark is a solid video; 60-80% signals an on-screen-text/hook issue. A retention dip below baseline at the 2-3 second (small accounts) or 5 second (larger) checkpoint means "focus on your hooks, full stop" (2025-10-15, 2026-01-02). Grant Owen's benchmark: hook rate above ~60% at three seconds correlates with much better Instagram performance (2025-12-31).
- **Add subtle visual movement in the first half-second.** A small motion (sliding books, adjusting the camera, moving the mic) acts as a scroll-stopper without distracting from the hook (2026-01-02). Visual anticipation baked into an object (something about to drop, shatter, get thrown) can carry the opening seconds (2025-10-29).
- **Frame zero must do work with no sound.** Before audio plays, the first visible frame (text plus image) has to stand alone; ~30% of TikTok viewers watch without audio, so captions are non-negotiable (2025-10-29, 2025-05-08).

## Common anti-patterns Briar warns against

- **Don't use hook swipe files or plug-and-play frameworks.** The internet moves too fast and strong "hooky" hooks trigger the opposite reaction; think in hook concepts (pain point, controversial take, dream scenario), not recycled lines (2025-10-17, 2025-10-20).
- **Don't over-engineer hooks to the point of inauthenticity.** "If a hook is too strong, it makes people feel the opposite about wanting to watch it." Briar predicts over-engineered, over-scripted hooks will earn automatic bounce rates within 2-3 years as audiences reject inauthenticity (2025-10-17, 2025-11-17, 2025-11-18).
- **Don't say "stop scrolling."** Downloadable hook packages that say "stop scrolling" get "no scroll" as the response (2025-09-10).
- **Don't use distasteful curiosity teases.** Lines like "the last tip is the most important" or "stay until the end" are overplayed, cost brand credibility, and get more people bouncing than staying (2025-10-24).
- **Don't confuse curiosity withholding with rage bait.** Leave out barely-unnecessary stuff, never the most important information; deliberately withholding the payoff to farm angry comments builds a terrible personal brand (2025-09-12, 2025-10-27).
- **Don't reverse the hook with words alone.** The Andrew Tate "climax line first" move works only with a strong visual payoff; with words alone, viewers just feel confused and scroll (2025-05-08).
- **Don't resolve the on-screen hook in the first ~15-20 seconds.** If the hook alludes to something later, make sure nothing early accidentally satisfies that curiosity; use brackets or extra clarity to redirect (2025-05-08).
- **Don't make declarative statements in on-screen text.** Statements assert a fact and close the loop; reframe as questions, contrasts, or relationships ("the difference between X and Y") so the viewer needs the video to resolve them (2025-05-08).
- **Don't over-explain with synonyms.** Reinforcement through examples is good; repeating the same idea three, four, five different ways is not (2025-10-23).
- **Don't use the red bouncing TikTok bubble or one-word/one-letter captions.** They signal beginner and are hard to read; Briar has more data that they don't work than that they do (2025-05-08).
- **Don't copy a hook until you can explain why it worked.** Copying is a crutch until you have the muscle to isolate the underlying psychological principle. Briar himself only copies a hook maybe 2 out of 10 times (2025-10-20).
- **Don't make the on-screen text too specific to cover only a slice of the video.** Go specific when you can, but if the hook only describes a 10-second slice, it's too narrow to represent the video's theme (2025-05-08).
- **Don't credit the back half of the video for what the first few seconds did.** When mining your outliers for buzzwords, pull from the on-screen text and first 3-4 seconds of verbal hook, since that's what most people actually saw (2025-10-03).
